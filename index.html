Working paper

Digital Media and the Market for News: Evidence from UK National Newspapers

Latest version: November 2026 · PDF

Abstract

I study whether mainstream news outlets shift their reporting language following salience shocks in the digital media environment. I apply an unsupervised text-scaling model (Text-Based Ideal Points) to the news corpora of The Guardian and The Daily Telegraph to estimate their relative ideological positions by biweek over 2013–2019. I then treat the most-read articles of The Canary, a digital-native, left-wing partisan outlet that rapidly gained prominence around Brexit and the 2017 General Election, as salience shocks and estimate short- and medium-term responses in mainstream outlets' ideological positioning. The Guardian shifts leftward (extremizes) following these articles, with effects concentrated in the first post-event weeks and decaying thereafter. A model of demand-driven spatial competition shows that an entrant's salience shock induces repositioning by the closest incumbent in a direction that depends on the pre-shock market configuration; The Guardian's move is the response of an incumbent defending a readership among which it was the majority choice. More broadly, the findings suggest that social media can affect mainstream news language by amplifying the reach of partisan content.

This work originated as my master's thesis at the University of Turin and Collegio Carlo Alberto.
